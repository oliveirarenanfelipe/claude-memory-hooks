# An inventory is not a deliverable

**The rule.** Cataloguing what exists is preparation. It looks like output — it is
long, it is organised, it took real effort — and it can be handed over in place of
the actual work without anyone immediately noticing, including you.

## The shape of the failure

The task was a plan of action. What was delivered was **192 items, listed,
grouped and titled** — and called a plan.

Every item was correct. The list was genuinely useful. It contained no decision
about what to do, in what order, or what to drop, which was the entire request.

*(Illustrative case.)*

## Why it happens to careful people specifically

Inventory is the part with no risk of being wrong. Every line is verifiable, the
work is visibly substantial, and it postpones the moment where you have to commit
to a judgement that could be criticised. Thoroughness becomes the hiding place.

There is also a real trap of sequence: the inventory genuinely is the first step,
so stopping there feels like stopping in the middle rather than stopping short.

## The test before handing it over

> **"What does the reader DO differently after reading this?"**

If the answer is "they now know what exists", it is an inventory. A deliverable
changes what happens next: it names what to do first, what to abandon, and what
the trade was.

## The fix is usually small

The inventory is not wasted — it is the input. What is missing is almost always
one short section at the top: the three things that matter, in order, with the
reason each one beats the alternatives, and what you are explicitly not doing.

That section is the deliverable. The 192 items are the appendix.
