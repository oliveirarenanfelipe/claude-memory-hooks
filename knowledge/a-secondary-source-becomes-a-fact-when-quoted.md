# A secondary source becomes a fact when quoted

**The rule.** A figure taken from a blog post, a talk or a summary enters your
text **looking identical** to a figure taken from primary documentation. Once it
is in a sentence, the distinction exists only in your memory — and any marker you
put beside it does not survive the next copy-paste.

## The shape of the failure

Two numbers from a well-written article go into a draft. The draft is reviewed,
tightened, and the numbers move into a message that will be sent to someone
outside.

Asked directly — *"are you completely sure about those?"* — neither survives
checking. One was from an older version of the product; the other was the
author's estimate, presented in a table alongside measured values.

Nothing was fabricated at any point. The provenance simply evaporated in transit.

## The rule that follows from where the text is going

**The standard rises with the audience.** Inside your own notes, a secondary
source is a useful lead. In something a person will *say out loud* — a client
call, an interview, a negotiation — every number must be one they can defend when
challenged, which means primary or verified.

The asymmetry is brutal: the cost of the number being right is zero, and the cost
of it being wrong is the credibility of everything said around it.

## The practice

1. When a number arrives from a secondary source, **follow the link once**, right
   then. Later never happens, and by then the marker is gone.
2. If the primary source cannot be reached, the sentence changes shape: *"reported
   around X"* instead of *"X"*. That is not weaker; it is accurate.
3. Before anything goes to a third party, re-ask every number: **what produced
   this?** If the honest answer is "an article", it does not go.
