# An item from a roundup is not a work item

**The rule.** A tool that shows up in a list, a thread or a roundup arrives
carrying **the value its author assumed** — which is always high, because that is
what the post exists to do. Measuring that it works, and that it is cheap,
answers *"can we use this?"*. It never answers **"what does it fix here that is
currently stuck?"** — and that is the question that decides.

## Why the mistake looks like diligence

You evaluate it properly: you check the real cost on a real account, you read the
licence at the source, you run it and time it. You write a verdict with evidence.

Every piece of that evidence is about **the tool**. None of it is about **the
pain**. It is an impeccable measurement on the wrong side of the question, and it
is much harder to spot than sloppiness, because rigour is present throughout.

## The shape of the failure

A scraper is evaluated and comes back excellent: ten businesses in fourteen
seconds, nine of ten with a phone number, half a cent each. All true. It becomes
a project.

Then someone asks: *"Is that phone number anything other than what anyone could
find on the internet? What exactly does this give me?"*

None of the sixty-two fields it returns says whether the business has the problem
being solved. The tool was never the constraint. The list of names was never what
was missing.

## The two questions, in order

1. **What is stuck today**, and what does the person do about it right now?
2. Does this tool remove *that*?

If you can answer the first only in general terms, you do not have a work item.
You have a catalogue entry — which is a perfectly good thing to have, filed
where it will surface when the subject comes up, and nothing more.

## The corollary

**The place for an evaluated tool is a catalogue with a trigger, not a plan.**
A tool that is genuinely useful will be found again by the problem it solves. A
tool promoted to a project because it impressed you will consume a week and be
abandoned.
