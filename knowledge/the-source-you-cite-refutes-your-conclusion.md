# The source you cite refutes the conclusion it supports

**The rule.** Before closing anything that argues a case with a list of sources,
read the list asking **which line contradicts the conclusion** — not whether each
source is real. Those are different audits, and only the second one catches this.

## The shape of the failure

A five-row table of citations supports a claim: a certain effect costs about 30%.
Every row is real, correctly quoted, with a working link. It went through three
reviews.

**Row four says 2%.**

It is not a mistake in the source. Row four measures a *different stage* of the
same phenomenon — the raw response, before a correction that exists precisely to
compensate for the gap the other rows describe. Read alone, it is correct. Sitting
in that table, under that conclusion, it hands the reader the refutation.

## Why three reviews missed it

1. **Every line was individually correct.** A source audit asks "does this
   citation exist?" — and it did. The failure is not in any row; it is in the
   relationship between rows.
2. **The table was read as a collection, not as an argument.** Accumulated
   citations become decoration for authority: the more rows, the more supported it
   looks, and the less anyone checks whether they agree with each other.
3. **The conclusion was written before the table** and never reconciled against it.
   This is the real cause. Nobody re-reads a conclusion they already believe.

## The cheap signal

**Two sources describing the same phenomenon with different orders of magnitude**
(2% against 30%). When that appears, one of three things is true and you must say
which: they measure different stages, they measure different populations, or one
of them is wrong. Never leave both in without saying.

## What it costs to be wrong

The reader who finds row four does not conclude that you made an error. They
conclude that your sources do not support you — using **your own citation**. That
is worse than having cited nothing.
