# One variable at a time, or the theory is free

**The rule.** When something is rejected and you do not know why, the temptation
is to explain it. The explanation arrives immediately, sounds coherent, and is
**wider than the evidence** — one data point supporting a general claim about how
things work.

Then you change three things at once, it improves, and the theory is confirmed by
something that proved nothing.

## The shape of the failure

Two outputs are rejected. Two theories are produced to explain them, each stated
confidently, each about a general property. Both are destroyed by the next test —
which also changed several things at once, so it produces a third theory.

The cost of the alternative was one extra run: change one parameter, keep the
rest fixed, and look.

*(Illustrative case.)*

## Why the broad theory is so attractive

Because it explains **both** rejections at once, which feels like more
understanding than an explanation covering one. Elegance is doing the persuading,
and elegance is not evidence.

## The two disciplines

**1. Vary one thing.** It is slower per iteration and dramatically faster to the
answer, because a result you cannot attribute is a result you have to get again.

**2. Say how wide your evidence is, in the same sentence as the claim.** *"On
these two cases, X was different"* is honest and useful. *"X is what matters"* is
a theory you have not earned, and it will steer the next ten decisions.

## The signal to watch for in yourself

You are explaining a rejection **before** you have looked at more than one
example. That is the moment. The correct next sentence is a question, not a
theory.
